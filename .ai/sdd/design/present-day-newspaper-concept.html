Compute & Energy · Concept analysis

Cheaper Intelligence, Larger Appetite

If every inference costs less, does the grid finally get relief—or does demand simply race ahead?

By the Knewzly history desk · Analysis

Vancouver — Illustrative scenario: AI labs and chipmakers celebrate another fall in the cost of running models. Yet the historical question is not whether each task uses less energy. It is whether cheaper use invites so much more use that total demand rises.

William Stanley Jevons described that tension in The Coal Question in 1865. More efficient steam engines, he argued, could expand coal consumption by lowering the effective cost of putting engines to work. Modern writers now use “Jevons paradox” as a lens for AI compute.

This lineage is an interpretation, not a claim that Victorian steam engines caused today’s data centers. It gives readers a sharper question to ask as Nvidia and model builders including Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, Meta AI, xAI and Mistral pursue greater efficiency.

National AI Bets Have a Precedent—and It Is Complicated

From the Knewzly policy wire

Illustrative scenario: Governments weigh new industrial-policy support for AI infrastructure and research. Japan’s Fifth Generation Computer Systems Project offers a less tidy precedent than victory-or-failure headlines allow.

Launched in 1982, the decade-long project was a national computing bet. It is widely regarded as a commercial failure that nonetheless advanced logic programming—useful history for evaluating present ambitions without flattening them into a morality tale.

Agents & Tools · Systems desk

Why the Agent Keeps Coming Back Around

By the Knewzly systems correspondent

Illustrative scenario: Tool-using agents turn repeated model calls into workflows. The crucial-looking novelty—a loop—has a deeper formal history.

The 1966 Böhm–Jacopini theorem established that sequence, selection and iteration are sufficient for structured computation. Linking it to today’s agent loops is a defensible interpretation, not a claim that the theorem anticipated language models.
